When a Principle Binds

Sessions 1–2 found that the five fairness principles barely differed, and that what difference there was fell inside the solver’s own indifference. This session moved the study to an operating point where the principles actually constrain the routing — and the constraint does something the earlier regime could not show.

The question

Was the problem the principles, or the operating point?

The earlier sessions measured five principles at the workshop tool’s own default settings and found almost nothing separating them. That result had two possible readings: the principles genuinely do the same thing on this map, or the default settings give them nothing to do. Distinguishing those is what this session is for, and the answer turns out to be the second.

FINDING 01

At the tool’s default settings, two of the five principles return the no-fairness routing exactly — not approximately.

Running each condition under six different aircraft orderings at the default setup, Equal and Sufficientarian produce the same peak added noise, the same maximum aircraft per tile, the same total added noise and the same trip lengths as the baseline, ordering for ordering, at N = 8. At N = 16 and 24 they differ, but by less than one condition varies on its own when only the aircraft ordering changes.

Prioritarian’s objective evaluated to exactly zero everywhere, and the reason was not the principle. Its protected set — the three loudest non-vertiport tiles — carries zero aircraft-timesteps in the baseline routing at every fleet size. Nobody was ever going to fly there, so protecting it cost nothing.

FINDING 02

Making every principle active needs two changes, and neither works alone.

A screen of 100 cells — five candidate setups, four conditions plus a control, four orderings each, all proven — tested each principle against what that principle is: a cap is active if it binds, an objective is active if switching it off changes what it is supposed to protect.

With the protected set on the corridor, the ablation control puts 3.45 dB on the protected communities and the objective removes it entirely. The two decibel objectives beat the baseline’s score on their own metric by more than the indifference floor (17.9 against 16.1, and 504.5 against 16.1). D6 was chosen over D5 on attributable differences at N = 24 — 2 of 6 metrics against 1 — and because the looser cap is likelier to stay solvable at the top of the ladder.

FINDING 03

A binding cap does not merely lower the peak noise — it removes the arbitrariness in it.

This is the result that did not exist in the earlier regime, and it is the one worth taking to the paper.

Every cell here is solved four times under four aircraft orderings. The orderings leave the problem mathematically identical — same fleet, same origin–destination pairs, same feasible set — so the spread across them is pure tie-break: which of many equally-optimal routings the solver happened to return. For the un-capped baseline that spread reaches 3.98 dB of peak added noise. For every capped condition, from N = 14 upward, it is exactly zero.

The capped conditions return a peak of 4.77 dB every single time from N = 14 on, while the baseline wanders between 5.64 and 7.70 dB depending on nothing anyone chose. Capacity utilisation sits at exactly 1.00, also with zero spread: the cap is binding on some community at some timestep, in all four orderings.

Why this matters beyond the instance: session 2 established that most differences between principles were smaller than the variation within one principle, and so could not be attributed to the principle. A binding constraint inverts that. It does not just move the outcome — it makes the outcome determinate, which is a property a community on the ground can actually rely on.

FINDING 04

Switching the two objectives to their count-weighted form makes the whole ladder solvable, at no cost in code.

Utilitarian and Proportional stall because both objectives are decibel-valued lookups: Z3 has to drive a table of dB expressions to its optimum and then prove it there. The solver already exposes a count-weighted form of each, described in the paper’s encoding section, selected by request parameters that cost no change to the solver at all.

FINDING 05

In the new regime the caps cost travel time and buy quieter peaks — attributably, from N = 22.

Across the whole of sessions 1–2, no principle ever flew further than the shortest-path floor except in a one-decibel window at the very edge of feasibility. At D = 6 that changes: from N = 22 all three cap-based conditions fly further than the no-fairness baseline and land a quieter peak, and both differences clear the indifference floor.

So the trade a fairness cap makes on this map is now visible and measurable: two to three fleet-hops of extra flying, for about two decibels off the worst-affected community’s peak, and for an outcome that no longer depends on which optimal routing the solver picked.

FINDING 06

Above N = 25 the two caps stop behaving alike, and one of them stops being solvable.

Above 25 aircraft the tool stops sending an absolute cap to Equal and sends a disparity cap instead — a bound on the spread between communities rather than on any one community’s level. At N = 30 that difference becomes decisive: Sufficientarian’s absolute cap still yields a verified routing within the one-hour harvest horizon, while all four Equal cells return no verifiable answer at all.

Not yet settled. N = 30 and 32 are still running and their cells are harvested rather than proven, so the numbers there are upper bounds on what the principle achieves, not the principle’s answer. Lane B has not started, so nothing is yet known about how the two objective principles behave on this setup above N = 16.

Method

What counts as a difference

Every claim above uses one test. A difference between two conditions counts only if their means differ by more than the larger of their two permutation bands and the bands do not overlap. The band is measured by re-solving the identical problem under different aircraft orderings, which leaves the feasible set and the optimum untouched and moves only which optimal routing comes back.

Only proven cells contribute to a band. A cell stopped at the harvest horizon sits wherever the search reached, which is not a sample from the optimal set, and averaging it in would widen the band and make every comparison look unattributable. Cells whose phase-2 search produced nothing carry the solver’s unweighted fallback route rather than the principle’s, and are excluded from the tables entirely.
